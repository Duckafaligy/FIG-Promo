Tipping when you use a restaurant deal: what to base it on, with the math

A 2 for 1 or a percent off changes your bill but not the work your server did. Here is how the math changes depending on whether you tip on the discounted total or the menu price.

Key takeaways

  • Tipping is between you and the restaurant, and FIG sets no tip amount.
  • Many diners tip on the menu price, because the service is the same with or without the deal. It is a choice, not a rule.
  • On a $44 order with a 2 for 1, a 15% tip is $3.90 on the $26 you pay or $6.60 on the $44 menu total.
  • Work out the tip before you leave home and add it to your budget.
  • A dine-in deal is paid to the restaurant directly, with no commission on the bill.

The short answer

There is no rule that says whether to tip on the discounted total or on the menu price, and FIG sets no tip amount. A common approach is to tip on what the order would have cost without the deal, because your server did the same work either way. If your budget is tight, tipping on the discounted total is also a choice many people make. Decide what you are comfortable with before you go.

This post shows the arithmetic both ways, so you can pick with the numbers in front of you. It is general information, not a rule.

Who decides

On FIG, a diner who uses a deal at the restaurant pays the restaurant directly, like any other visit. Restaurants set their own deals, and the tip is between you and the restaurant. FIG does not take a share of the bill for dine-in and does not set a tip.

That means the question is really about your own budget and your own view of fairness. The rest of this post helps you compare the options.

The same order, tipped two ways

These are round example numbers, not any real menu. Two of you order two mains at $18 each and two drinks at $4 each, so $36 + $8 = $44.00 before tax. A 2 for 1 on mains means one main is free, so you pay $18 + $8 = $26.00.

A $44 order with a 2 for 1 (example, before tax)
Tip as a share of10%15%18%20%
Discounted total, $26.00$2.60$3.90$4.68$5.20
Menu price, $44.00$4.40$6.60$7.92$8.80

Check: 15% of $26 is $3.90, and 15% of $44 is $6.60. The difference at 15% is $6.60 − $3.90 = $2.70. At 20% it is $8.80 − $5.20 = $3.60. With a 15% tip on the menu price, you would pay $26.00 + $6.60 = $32.60 before tax, against $44.00 + $6.60 = $50.60 with no deal.

The deal still saves you $18.00 in this example, even if you tip on the full menu price. That is why many people treat the deal as a saving on the food and keep the tip where it would have been.

Why some people tip on the menu price

  • The work is the same. The server carries the same plates, refills the same drinks and keeps the table the same way.
  • The restaurant chose the deal, not the server. The discount is the restaurant's decision, so the server's tip should not shrink because of it.
  • It is simple. You can read the menu prices and work the tip out before you order.

None of that makes it an obligation. If you are watching your budget, you can plan for the tip you can afford.

Why some people tip on the discounted total

  • Budget. A deal is often the reason someone can eat out at all.
  • The bill is what you are paying. A tip on the amount that leaves your wallet is easy to work out at the table.
  • It is still a tip. A smaller tip on a smaller bill is still money for good service.

Both views are reasonable. If you tip on the discounted total, consider rounding up, since a small round-up costs little.

Plan the tip before you go

The easiest way to avoid an awkward moment at the table is to settle the number at home. Look at the restaurant's menu on its FIG page, add up what you expect to order, and write down the tip you plan to leave. A two-minute check of a restaurant on FIG shows what to look at first.

  1. Add up the menu prices of what you will order.
  2. Subtract the deal, using its exact wording.
  3. Decide your tip, and on which number.
  4. Add tax and the tip to get your real total.

If you are splitting the bill with friends, eating out as a group covers how to divide it.

When the deal is a percent off

The same logic holds for a percent off. Say the bill is $60.00 and the deal is 20% off, which is $12.00, so you pay $48.00. A 15% tip is $9.00 on $60, or $7.20 on $48. The gap is $1.80. Percent deals make the gap proportional to the discount, while a 2 for 1 can make it larger on a table of mostly mains.

Check

20% of $60 is $12.00, and $60 − $12 = $48.00. 15% of $60 is $9.00, and 15% of $48 is $7.20. $9.00 − $7.20 = $1.80.

A table of four, splitting the bill

With a group, the tip question becomes a splitting question. Say four of you have $100.00 of food and drinks at menu prices, and a 20% off deal takes $20.00 off, so the bill is $80.00. These are round example numbers, before tax.

Tip on15% tipTotalEach of four
Menu price, $100.00$15.00$95.00$23.75
Discounted bill, $80.00$12.00$92.00$23.00

Check: $80 + $15 = $95, and $95 ÷ 4 = $23.75. $80 + $12 = $92, and $92 ÷ 4 = $23.00. The difference is $0.75 a person, which most groups can agree on without a long talk.

What if the deal is free with a dish?

A free-with-a-dish deal changes the bill in a different way. Say a main is $18.00 and a side that normally costs $6.00 comes free with it. You pay $18.00 instead of $24.00. A 15% tip is $3.60 on the $24.00 menu total, or $2.70 on the $18.00 you pay. The gap is $0.90, and it comes from a free item rather than a discount on a dish. The same options apply: tip on the menu total, or on what you pay.

Check

$18.00 + $6.00 = $24.00. 15% of $24 is $3.60, and 15% of $18 is $2.70. $3.60 − $2.70 = $0.90.

A quick way to do it in your head

You do not need a calculator for a tip at the table. Work out 10% by moving the decimal one place, then add half of that for 15%, or double it for 20%. On $44.00, 10% is $4.40, half of that is $2.20, and so 15% is $6.60. On $26.00, 10% is $2.60, half is $1.30, so 15% is $3.90. The same trick works on any total, and it keeps the conversation at the table on the food rather than the bill.

Takeout is different

For takeout ordered in the app, you pay online at checkout, the card is held and charged when the restaurant accepts, and there is no paying at the counter. This post is about dine-in. For how a takeout order works, see ordering takeout on FIG.

Be kind if something goes wrong

If a deal does not scan or a dish is missing, the person helping you is rarely the one who set it up. A calm word and a thank-you go a long way. Deal not working at the counter? has the usual causes and what to say.

Questions

Should I tip on the discounted total or the menu price?

There is no rule. Many diners tip on the menu price because the service is the same, and others tip on the discounted total to fit a budget. It is your choice.

Does FIG set a tip?

No. For dine-in, you pay the restaurant directly and the tip is between you and the restaurant.

Does FIG take a share of my dine-in bill?

No. FIG takes no commission on the bill and nothing on dine-in.

How much does a 2 for 1 change the tip?

In the example, a 15% tip is $3.90 on the $26.00 you pay or $6.60 on the $44.00 menu total, a difference of $2.70.

Can I work out the tip before I go?

Yes. Use the menu prices on the restaurant's FIG page, subtract the deal, and decide which number to tip on.

Is this tax or legal advice?

No. It is general information about a common question.
