Menu photos with a phone: how to photograph your dishes for your FIG menu

Your FIG menu has photos and sections, and photos are what diners look at first. How to shoot your dishes with the phone you already own: light, angle, plating, which dishes to do first and how long it takes.

Key takeaways

  • A FIG menu has photos and sections, and you add both yourself. You do not need a studio or a hired photographer.
  • Daylight from a window, a clean plate and a slightly raised angle do most of the work.
  • Photograph your best sellers first. A menu with eight good photos beats one with thirty rushed ones.
  • Shoot the dish you really serve, at the size you really serve it, so the plate that arrives matches the picture.
  • Budget about four minutes a dish. Twenty dishes is a little over an hour, which fits into one quiet afternoon.

The short answer

You can take good menu photos with the phone in your pocket. Put the plate by a window in daylight, wipe the rim, hold the phone a little above the dish and take three or four shots, then keep the best one. Start with your best sellers, because those are the dishes diners will look for first on your FIG menu.

Why photos matter on a menu page

On FIG, diners will browse places, menus, reviews and live deals on a map. A menu with photos and sections is easier to read at a glance than a long list of names, and a photo answers the questions a diner would otherwise have to ask: how big is it, what is on it, does it look like what I want tonight?

A photo is also a promise. If the picture shows a full bowl and the bowl that arrives is half that, the diner remembers the gap, and so does the review. The best menu photo is an honest one.

Light: the one thing worth getting right

Most bad food photos are bad because of light, not the phone. A few rules:

  • Use daylight. Stand the dish near a window, with the light coming from the side or slightly behind the plate.
  • Turn off the overhead lights in that corner. Mixed light makes food look yellow or grey.
  • Skip the flash. It flattens the dish and makes sauce look oily.
  • Shoot at the right time of day. If your kitchen is busy at noon, shoot at mid-afternoon when the room is quiet and the light is still good.

If your restaurant has no good window, a spot just inside the front door often works, before opening.

Angle and framing

Different dishes want different angles:

Kind of dishTry this angle
Flat dishes: pizza, a platter, a bowl seen from the topStraight down
Tall dishes: burgers, stacked sandwiches, layered dessertsFrom the side, at the height of the dish
Most plates and bowlsA slightly raised angle, about halfway between the two
DrinksFrom the side, so the colour and the glass show

Keep the dish in the middle with a little empty space around it, and use the same angle for dishes of the same kind. A menu where the burgers all share one angle looks tidy, and diners compare them more easily.

Plating for the camera, without cheating

  • Wipe the rim of the plate or bowl. Sauce drips are the most common flaw.
  • Use the real portion. Do not pile it higher than a customer would receive.
  • Add fresh colour that you would really serve, such as a few chopped green onions or a lemon wedge.
  • Shoot it fast. Fries wilt and sauces skin over, so have the camera ready before the plate comes out.
  • Keep plates and bowls consistent across the menu if you can.

What to photograph first

Do not try to shoot the whole menu on day one. Work in this order:

  1. Your best sellers. If you know what moves most, start there.
  2. The dishes you want to sell more of, especially any you will use in a deal.
  3. One dish per section, so every part of the menu has at least one picture.
  4. The rest, whenever you have time.

If you are about to post a deal, photograph the dish it applies to first. A deal on a dish with a good photo is easier for a diner to say yes to.

A time budget, with the sum

How long it takes (example)
  • Four minutes a dish: a minute to plate, two to shoot three or four angles, a minute to pick the best.
  • Eight best sellers: 8 × 4 = 32 minutes.
  • Twenty dishes: 20 × 4 = 80 minutes, which is one hour and twenty minutes.

These are example numbers. Your own pace may be faster, because the second and third dish go quicker than the first.

If you have a staff member who is good with a phone, ask them to run the session. People who photograph their own friends' dinners often take the best menu photos.

Choosing the best shot

Take three or four photos of each dish and then compare them side by side. Choose the one where the food is sharp and the plate is clean. Delete the rest, so the wrong one never ends up on your menu by mistake. Crop if you need to, but leave the edit there: heavy filters change the colour of the food, and then the dish on the table does not match.

When a dish changes, whether the recipe, the garnish or the plate, take a new photo. A photo that is a year old and no longer true is worse than no photo at all.

Putting the photos on your FIG menu

In your restaurant account, your menu is built from sections with dishes in them, and each dish can have a photo. Group dishes the way your printed menu does, add the photo to each dish and check that every price is right. Our guide to setting up your FIG menu and takeout in 15 minutes goes through the screens. If you want the full list of what to do before you go live, see the restaurant checklist.

Diners are on FIG now, so a photo starts working as soon as your menu is published. Do your most popular dishes first and the rest at a calm pace.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Stock photos. They never match what you serve.
  • Dark photos. If you cannot see the food on a phone screen in the sun, it is too dark.
  • Hands and thumbs at the edge of the frame.
  • A different look on every dish. Consistency reads as care.
  • Leaving a dish without a photo because it is hard to shoot. Show the plate as it is, steam and all.

Questions

Do I need a professional photographer for my FIG menu?

No. A phone, a window with daylight and a clean plate are enough. Start with your best sellers.

Does my FIG menu need photos?

A FIG menu has photos and sections. You add them yourself, and you can start with your best sellers and add the rest later.

What is the best light for food photos?

Daylight from a window, coming from the side or slightly behind the plate. Turn off overhead lights and skip the flash.

How many photos should I take of each dish?

Three or four from slightly different angles, then keep the best one and delete the rest.

Should the photo show the real portion?

Yes. Show the dish as you serve it, so that the plate that reaches the table matches the picture.

How long does it take to photograph a menu?

About four minutes a dish as an example, so twenty dishes take a little over an hour. Do the best sellers first.

Can diners see my menu now?

Yes. FIG is open to diners in Markham, and they see your menu and photos as soon as they are published.
