Regulars using your deal: what it costs, and what to do about it

Some of the people scanning your deal would have come anyway. Here is the arithmetic on a 20-scan week, the point where a deal still pays, and the settings that tilt it toward the hours you want to fill.

Key takeaways

  • Some discount going to regulars is normal. The question is whether the new visits cover it.
  • In the 20-scan example below, 8 new visits and 12 regulars leave about $20.80 after the discount.
  • With 12 regulars using a $4-off deal, it takes 6 new visits at the same bill to break even.
  • Aim the deal at the hours you are actually quiet, so fewer of the scans are visits you already had.
  • Uses per diner and the wait between uses control how often one person can come back to it.

The short answer

Yes, regulars will use your deal, and some of the discount goes to visits you would have had anyway. That is only a problem if the new visits do not cover it. The arithmetic below shows where the line sits on a typical week, and which settings move more of the deal toward the hours you want to fill.

Why this is worth working out

A deal has one job: bring people in when the room is quiet. Every scan is either a visit you would not have had, or a visit you would have had at full price. The first kind adds money. The second kind costs you the discount.

You cannot sort people perfectly, and you should not try. What you can do is work out how many new visits you need before the deal pays for itself, and then aim the deal so that number is easy to hit.

A 20-scan week, with the numbers

These are round example numbers. Put your own average bill and food cost in and the shape of the answer stays the same.

A $4-off deal, 20 scans in a week (example)
  • Average bill before the deal: $18. Food cost: 30% of the menu price, so $5.40 a visit.
  • Each new visit brings in $18 − $4 = $14, less $5.40 of food, so $8.60 toward wages, rent and profit.
  • Each regular who would have come anyway costs you the $4 you took off.
  • Say 8 of the 20 were new: 8 × $8.60 = $68.80.
  • The other 12 cost 12 × $4 = $48.
  • Net: $68.80 − $48 = $20.80 better off than running nothing.

Check the middle step: a $14 payment with $5.40 of food on the plate leaves $8.60. Food cost of 28% to 32% of menu price is the range usually quoted for restaurants, and 30% is the middle of it.

Note what the sum does not count: the regulars still paid for their meals. The $48 is only the discount, not the whole bill.

The break-even line

With 12 regulars scanning a $4-off deal, the discount costs $48. Each new visit brings $8.60. $48 ÷ $8.60 = 5.58, so it takes 6 new visits to break even. Beyond that, every new visit is money you did not have.

With a $2-off deal the discount would be $24 and each new visit would bring $10.60, so you would need 3 new visits. A smaller discount is easier to cover, but it also pulls fewer people in, which is why the next section is about aim rather than size.

Aim the deal, then size it

If a deal runs at your busiest hour, most scans will be people who were coming anyway, and the table above goes red. The fix is aim.

  • Schedule it for the hours you are genuinely quiet. A deal can be live now or scheduled. Live now or scheduled goes through the choice.
  • Pick a dish that travels well with a new diner. Something a first-timer would order anyway, with a decent margin.
  • Use a 2 for 1 when you want a second person. It needs two people, which usually means a regular bringing someone who has never been in.
  • Set uses per diner and a wait between uses. One person can use a deal up to 20 times if you allow it, with an optional wait in between, so you choose how often a regular can come back to the same discount.
  • Leave your peak alone. There is nothing to fill.

Seven ways to fill slow hours has more ideas for the quiet end of the day, and the slow-hour math works through the break-even for a single hour.

Why some discount to regulars is fine

A regular who uses your deal is not a loss to be stopped. Three things come back the other way:

  • Visits in hours they would not normally come. A regular who comes on a Tuesday afternoon because the deal runs then is filling the hour you wanted filled.
  • Reviews from real visits. Reviews come only from scanned visits, so the deal is also how a quiet restaurant builds a review history. Deals get likes, and restaurants get stars.
  • The second person. Regulars bring people. The friend is the new diner you were paying for.

What you want to avoid is the opposite case: the same few people taking the same discount every day at the hours you were full anyway.

How to tell what you are getting

FIG shows results deal by deal, so you can see how each deal did on its own. Whether a face is new is something you and your team can judge at the till, and it is worth asking for two weeks. A short note by the register, one mark for a first visit, is enough to put a real number into the table above.

Give a deal a couple of weeks before judging it, and change one thing at a time so you know what caused a difference. Reading your deal results covers the keep, change or stop decision.

Three changes worth trying, one at a time

  1. Move the hours. Shift the deal an hour earlier or later and see whether the scans follow. If they do not, the hour was not the problem.
  2. Add a wait between uses. If a handful of people are scanning most days, a wait spreads the same discount over more people.
  3. Change the dish, not the discount. Moving a $4-off deal from a $25 dish to an $18 dish changes how it reads to a new diner, without costing you more.

If you want to compare a dollar off with a per cent off or a 2 for 1 before changing anything, pricing a restaurant deal sets them side by side.

Questions

Is it bad if my regulars use my deal?

Not in itself. The discount to people who would have come anyway is the cost; the new visits are the return. Work out whether the second covers the first.

How many new visits do I need to break even?

Divide the discount given to regulars by what one new visit leaves you. In the example, $48 divided by $8.60 is 5.58, so 6 new visits.

How do I stop one person using a deal every day?

Set uses per diner, and a wait between uses. One diner can use a deal up to 20 times if you allow it, and a diner can claim a deal once a day.

Should I just make the discount smaller?

A smaller discount is easier to cover, but it pulls fewer people in. Aim the deal at a quiet hour first, then adjust the size.

Can I see which scans were new diners?

FIG shows results deal by deal. Whether a face is new is a judgement you and your team make at the till, and a note by the register for two weeks gives you a number.

How long should I run a deal before deciding?

A couple of weeks. Change one thing at a time so you know what caused a difference.

Does FIG take a cut of a dine-in bill?

No. There is no commission on the bill and none on dine-in. FIG keeps 5% of the food only on takeout orders paid in the app.
