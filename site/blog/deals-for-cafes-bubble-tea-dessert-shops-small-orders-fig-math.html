Deals for cafés, bubble tea and dessert shops: small orders and the math

When the average order is a drink and a snack, a flat card fee weighs more. Here is how deals and takeout work out on $4 to $16 of food, and how to build an order that keeps more.

Key takeaways

  • Dine-in deals cost a restaurant nothing to FIG. Fees apply only to takeout paid in the app.
  • On takeout, FIG keeps 5% of the food after any deal and before tax, and Stripe's 2.9% + $0.30 comes off the payment.
  • The flat $0.30 matters most on small orders: about 15.8% of a $4 order but 10.1% of a $16 order.
  • A drink plus a snack at one price moves a small order toward a bigger one.
  • A restaurant can set 0 deals per order, so takeout is at full price.

The short answer

If your average order is a drink and something small, put your deal on dine-in first, where FIG takes nothing, and build takeout around combinations so each order is larger. On takeout, the flat $0.30 in Stripe's fee weighs more on small orders: in the example below, the two fees take about 15.8% of $4.00 of food but about 10.1% of $16.00.

The numbers are examples with round prices. Your own statement shows your real orders.

Four small orders, side by side

The food is the amount charged for dishes after any deal and before tax. The diner pays that plus 13% HST. Stripe's percentage applies to the whole payment, tax included. FIG's 5% is on the food only. These are round example orders.

Check the $10.00 row: HST is $1.30, so the payment is $11.30. Stripe takes 2.9% of $11.30, which is $0.33, plus $0.30, so $0.63. FIG takes 5% of $10.00, which is $0.50. $10.00 − $0.63 − $0.50 = $8.87. Fees are rounded to the cent. Tax on very small sales can differ in Ontario, and HST on the order is the restaurant's to remit, so treat the tax line as an example and use your statement for real figures.

The pattern is clear. The fixed $0.30 is the same on every order, so it weighs most when the order is smallest. Takeout order math works through larger orders in the same way.

Contribution matters more than the fee

What counts is what is left after your ingredients and cups. Take a $10.00 order of food with a cost of 30%, so $3.00. You keep $8.87 and the contribution is $8.87 − $3.00 = $5.87 toward wages and rent. Compare that with an empty counter at 3 pm: $0. A small order in a quiet hour can still be worth taking.

Use your own numbers. Cafés and bubble tea shops often have different costs from a kitchen, so write down the real cost of your drink with its cup, lid and toppings.

Put the deal where it costs least

  • Dine-in first. A deal at the counter costs FIG nothing, so a dollar-off or a free topping works with no per-order fee.
  • A deal on the add-on. A free pastry with a drink at full price makes the order bigger, not smaller. A "free with a dish" deal is built for that. Free with a dish, with the math has the arithmetic.
  • Quiet hours. An afternoon slot is where an idle counter and an available cook cost you least. The slow-hour math shows how to test it.

Build an order, not a drink

Takeout comes with a deal, unless you set 0 deals per order, in which case it is at full price. For small orders, the second choice deserves a look. A full-price takeout order has no discount to pay for, so more of each dollar stays with you.

You can also make the basket bigger. A drink plus a snack at one set price turns a $6.00 order into a $10.00 one, and in the table that moves the fee share from 13.3% to 11.3%. The food you add is food you sold at full price, which is the bigger gain.

Example: a combo

A $6.00 drink and a $4.00 pastry sold together as a $10.00 combo is the $10.00 row in the table: you keep $8.87. The same drink alone, as a $6.00 order, leaves $5.20. So the combo keeps $3.67 more than the drink alone, and $8.87 − $5.20 = $3.67 is the number to compare with the cost of the pastry.

When the line is long

Small-order shops feel a rush quickly. If the line at the counter is long, you can pause takeout and add prep time, so app orders do not stack up behind the people in front of you. Takeout during a rush covers how.

A quiet-hour deal on a drink, with the math

Here is a dine-in example with round numbers. A drink sells for $6.00, the cup, lid and ingredients cost about $1.80 (30%), and you offer $1.00 off between 2 and 4 pm on weekdays. FIG takes nothing from a dine-in bill.

A small discount on a small item is easy to afford, which is why a short window and a tight limit matter. Regulars using your deal shows how to tell which case you are in.

Small shops and big queues

A small shop often has one or two people behind the counter. A deal that sends five people in at once can slow every order. Keep the deal to the hours you can handle, pick a limit on uses that suits the counter, and brief the person on the till. The scan takes a moment, and staff scan with any phone or tablet. Train your staff to honour a FIG deal covers it in ten minutes.

Think too about what you want diners to buy next. A deal on a drink can bring someone who then buys a pastry at full price. A deal on the pastry may do the opposite. Start with the item whose margin is best and the one you most want to be known for.

What you need for takeout

Takeout paid in the app is available only after a restaurant has finished Stripe's checks and payouts and added its HST number. Stripe, not FIG, checks ID and holds ID and bank details. Dine-in deals need none of that, so you can start there. Do you need Stripe to go live on FIG? explains the split. HST registration is general information, not advice, and HST registration for small restaurants is a good place to start.

A short plan

  1. Start with a dine-in deal in your quietest window.
  2. Write down the real cost of your three most popular items.
  3. Create one combo that lifts the average order.
  4. Decide whether takeout runs with a deal or at full price.
  5. After two weeks, open your deal results and keep what works.

Questions

Does FIG charge a café anything for dine-in deals?

No. FIG takes no commission on the bill and nothing on dine-in. Posting deals costs nothing.

What does FIG keep on takeout?

5% of the food after any deal and before tax, on takeout orders paid in the app, plus HST on that fee once FIG is registered for HST.

Why do small orders cost more in percentage terms?

Stripe's card fee has a flat $0.30 part. In the example, the fees are about 15.8% of $4.00 of food and 10.1% of $16.00.

Can I sell takeout at full price?

Yes. Set 0 deals per order and takeout is at full price.

Who pays Stripe's fee?

The restaurant. Stripe's 2.9% + $0.30 for most Canadian cards comes off the payment.

Can I pause takeout when the line is long?

Yes. A restaurant can pause takeout and add prep time.

Do I need Stripe to post a dine-in deal?

No. Stripe's checks are needed only to turn on takeout paid in the app.
