A monthly eating-out budget that works: where FIG deals fit in Markham

Set one number for eating out, split it into a few kinds of meals, add HST and tip, and see where a deal fits. A worked example with round numbers, and what to expect from FIG in Markham.

Key takeaways

  • Pick one monthly number for eating out and split it into a few kinds of meals, so one big dinner does not use it all.
  • Count HST and tip: a $40 meal with 13% HST and a 15% tip is about $51.20 in your wallet.
  • A deal helps most on the meals you were going to have anyway, not on extra ones.

  • Review the month in five minutes at the end, and move the number up or down.

The short answer

Choose one number for eating out each month, then divide it into three or four kinds of meal: quick lunches, relaxed dinners, treats and takeout. Work out each meal as it will appear on your card, with HST and tip, not as the menu price. On FIG, you can look for deals on the meals you were going to have anyway.

Start with one number

Most people overspend on eating out because they have no number. Look at the last two or three months of card statements, add up what went to restaurants, cafes and takeout, and take the average. Then choose a figure for next month that you are comfortable with, even if it is a little lower. A number you can keep to is better than a perfect number you ignore.

Write it as a monthly figure and a weekly one. In an example, $240 a month is about $60 a week, which is easier to hold in your head on a Friday evening.

What a menu price really costs

The price on the menu is not what you pay. Ontario's HST is 13%, and many people tip on top. Here is a round example, with a 15% tip on the pre-tax amount. The tip is an example, not a rule.

A $40 meal, all in (example)
LineAmount
Menu price$40.00
HST at 13%$5.20
Tip at 15% of $40$6.00
Total$51.20

So a $40 meal costs about 28% more than the menu says: $51.20 − $40.00 = $11.20, and $11.20 ÷ $40.00 = 0.28.

Do your budget with the all-in number. Otherwise a month of ten $40 meals is $400 on paper and $512 in real life.

Split the number into kinds of meal

Here is an example split for $240 a month. Yours will differ, and the figures are round for easy checking.

Kind of mealHow manyAll-in eachTotal
Quick lunch6$15$90
Casual dinner2$35$70
Takeout night2$25$50
One treat1$30$30
Total11$240

Check the sum: $90 + $70 + $50 + $30 = $240. Eleven meals out in a month is a little under three a week.

Where a deal fits

A deal is useful when it lowers the price of a meal you were already going to have. It is not useful when it talks you into an extra one. On FIG, you can browse places, menus, reviews and live deals on a map, and claim a deal in one tap. A diner can claim a deal once a day, so it suits regular meals better than a binge.

A 2 for 1 on a regular dinner (example)
  • Two mains at $18 each, normally $36 before HST and tip.
  • With a 2 for 1, you pay for one: $18.
  • You saved $18 on that meal, which is half the bill.

If your casual-dinner line has room for it, the saving could pay for another lunch. Check the real terms of each deal, because every restaurant sets its own.

Three habits that make the number stick

  1. Choose the treat. A big dinner you looked forward to feels better than three small ones you forgot. Put it in the budget on purpose.
  2. Keep a running tally on your phone's notes, once a meal. It takes ten seconds.
  3. Check before you go. Look at the menu, the price and any deal first. It is easier to decide at home than at the table.

Lunch, dinner and takeout: where the money goes

It helps to see which kind of meal costs you the most per dollar of enjoyment. A lunch out at $15 all-in is easy to repeat and easy to skip. A dinner at $35 is a bigger decision. A takeout night at $25 costs less than a dinner out but more than cooking.

On FIG, takeout is paid for online in the app at checkout, and it comes with a deal unless the restaurant sets takeout at full price. Whether or not a deal is on that night, check the price you see at checkout before you confirm, and put the total into your tally. There is no paying at the counter for takeout, so the amount in the app is the amount you spend.

Swapping one meal (example)
  • Before: two takeout nights at $25 each is $50.
  • If one of them becomes a lunch at $15, you spend $25 + $15 = $40.
  • That leaves $10 spare, which is two-thirds of another lunch.

A five-minute month-end review

On the last day of the month, add up what you spent and compare it with the number. Ask two questions: which kind of meal went over, and which one gave you the most enjoyment for the money? Then move $10 or $20 from one line to the other. After two or three months, the budget fits how you actually eat.

If you eat out far less than your number, that is fine too. The point of the number is to choose, not to spend it.

A note on tipping

Tipping is up to you and to the service you had. The 15% in the example is only there to make the arithmetic concrete. Use whatever you normally leave. Whatever it is, add it to your all-in figures, so your number stays honest.

Questions

How much should I budget for eating out each month?

There is no right figure. Add up the last two or three months of card statements, take the average and choose a number you are comfortable with, even if it is a little lower.

Should I count HST and tip in my restaurant budget?

Yes. Ontario's HST is 13%, and tips come on top. In the example, a $40 meal comes to about $51.20 with HST and a 15% tip.

Which deals save the most?

The ones on meals you would have had anyway. A 2 for 1 on two $18 mains saves $18, half of that bill, but only if you did not add a meal you would have skipped.
