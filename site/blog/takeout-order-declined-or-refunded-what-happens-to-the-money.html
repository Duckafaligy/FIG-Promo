Takeout order declined or refunded: what happens to the money on FIG

A takeout order is held on the diner's card and charged when you accept. What that means if you decline, if you need to refund, who can do it, and where to see every refund and fee afterwards.

Key takeaways

  • If the restaurant declines, nothing is charged. The kitchen starts only when you accept.
  • Refunds are a money step, so owners and managers need their stronger sign-in to make one.
  • The Payments page lists every takeout payment, refund and fee, and downloads as a CSV file.
  • FIG keeps 5% of the food after any deal and before tax, on takeout orders only. Use your Payments page, not memory, to see what a refunded order did to your month.

The short answer

When a diner pays for takeout in the FIG app, the card is held and is charged when the restaurant accepts the order. If you decline, nothing is charged. If you need to give money back after accepting, that is a refund, and owners and managers need their stronger sign-in to do it. Every payment, refund and fee then shows on your Payments page.

The life of a takeout order

StepWhat happensMoney
1. The diner checks outThey pay online in the app. There is no paying at the counterThe card is held
2a. You acceptThe kitchen startsThe card is charged
2b. You declineThe order does not go aheadNothing is charged
3. The diner collectsStaff use the pickup code to confirm collectionNo new money moves. The code only confirms collection
4. Something goes wrong laterYou refund the orderListed on your Payments page

Two things in this table are worth remembering. The kitchen starts when you accept, so accept only when you can make the order. And the pickup code is not a payment step. By the time the diner arrives, the payment has already happened.

Declining an order

You can decline an order that you cannot make: the kitchen is out of the main ingredient, the dish is sold out, or it is simply too busy. Because the card is only held until you accept, a declined order costs the diner nothing, and you have lost no fee.

It is far better to decline quickly than to accept and then fail to deliver. A diner who is told no can eat somewhere else. A diner whose order is accepted and then late or wrong is the one who leaves a poor review. If you decline often at the same hour, that is the sign to use pause or prep time. The rush guide covers both.

Refunding an order

Once you have accepted and the card has been charged, a refund is how money goes back. Reasons you might refund include a dish that could not be made after you accepted, a mistake in the kitchen, or a problem the diner raised at pickup.

Refunds are one of the money steps. Owners and managers need their stronger sign-in for them, which is an authenticator app or a code by email. Money steps stay with the people who answer for them. The roles and sign-in guide shows how to choose who has which role.

Where you see it afterwards

Your Payments page lists every takeout payment, refund and fee, and it downloads as a CSV file. When an order is refunded, it shows there as its own line. Use it to answer three questions at month end:

  1. How much did I take in takeout payments?
  2. How much did I refund?
  3. What did the fees come to?

Our guide to reading the Payments page goes through the lines in detail and shows how to hand the file to a bookkeeper.

Keeping the refund count low

A refund is a symptom. These steps stop most of them before they start:

  • Keep your menu current. A dish that is not available should not be on the menu. Check photos and prices at the start of each week.
  • Add prep time when the kitchen is slow, so pickup times are honest.
  • Pause takeout when you cannot keep up.
  • Set deals per order so one order cannot stack more discounts than you meant.
  • Check each bag against the order before it leaves the counter.

What a refund can cost you: a worked example

This example uses round numbers, not a real restaurant. Suppose one month holds 40 takeout orders of $30 of food each, and 2 of them are refunded in full.

Two refunds in forty orders (example)
  • Takeout food sold: 40 × $30 = $1,200.
  • Refunded: 2 × $30 = $60, which is 5% of the month's takeout food.
  • Food still sold: $1,200 − $60 = $1,140.

The Payments page shows what happened to the fees on each refunded order. Look there rather than guessing.

The point of the example is the rate. Two refunds in forty orders is one in twenty. If you see one in five, the problem is upstream, in the menu, the prep time or the staffing, and the fix is cheaper than the refunds.

A busy-night routine that prevents most problems

Most declined and refunded orders come from the same few causes, and they are cheap to prevent. Before a busy service, run through this short list with whoever is on the pass:

  1. Check what is sold out. Take it off the menu before the first order arrives, not after.
  2. Set the prep time. If the kitchen is running 25 minutes behind, say so in the prep time rather than promising a quick pickup.
  3. Decide who watches new orders. One person should own the accept and decline buttons, so an order never sits unseen.
  4. Know when you will pause. Agree a point, such as more tickets on the line than you can fire at once, at which takeout is paused until the line clears.

None of this costs anything, and each step protects the one thing a refund cannot repair, which is the diner's trust.

What the diner sees

From the diner's side, the order is simple. They pay online at checkout, they wait to hear whether the restaurant accepts, and they collect with a pickup code that confirms the handover. They are never asked to pay at the counter. That is why a decline is painless for them: nothing was taken. It is also why you should decline fast. The sooner the answer comes, the sooner they can order somewhere else.

Talking to the diner

A refund goes better with a short, plain note. Say what went wrong, say what you did and say you are sorry. Do not argue about it. A diner who gets a quick refund and an apology will often order again. A diner who is made to prove a point rarely does.

If the problem was in the review, answer it. Replying to reviews has templates that are easy to adapt.

What to do before takeout is on

A restaurant can turn takeout on only after it has added its HST number and finished Stripe payouts, and those steps ask owners and managers for the stronger sign-in. Do them early, so that a refund on your first busy day is not the first time you meet the sign-in. Takeout is optional, and FIG earns only on takeout orders paid in the app. Dine-in has no such fee.

Questions

When is the diner's card charged for takeout?

The card is held at checkout and charged when the restaurant accepts the order.

What if I decline a takeout order?

Nothing is charged. The kitchen starts when the restaurant accepts.

Who can refund a takeout order?

Refunds are a money step, so owners and managers need their stronger sign-in to make one.

Where do I see my refunds and fees?

On the Payments page, which lists every takeout payment, refund and fee and downloads as a CSV file.

Can a diner pay at the counter for takeout?

No. Diners pay online in the app at checkout. The pickup code only confirms collection.

What does FIG keep on a takeout order?

5% of the food after any deal and before tax, plus HST on that fee once FIG is registered for HST. Stripe's card fee of 2.9% + $0.30 for most Canadian cards comes off the payment.

Do I pay anything on dine-in orders?

No. There is no commission on the bill and nothing on dine-in.
