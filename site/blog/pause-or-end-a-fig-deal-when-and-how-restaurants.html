When to pause or end a deal on FIG, and how to do it without upsetting diners

Sometimes a deal should stop: an ingredient costs more, the kitchen is short-handed, or you will be closed. Here is when to pause or end one, and how to keep diners from arriving to a surprise.

Key takeaways

  • Pause or end a deal when the maths turns thin, when you cannot serve it, or when you will be closed.
  • A claim lasts as long as the deal runs, and it ends only when the restaurant ends the deal, or a flash or dated deal is over.
  • In the example, a $0.80 rise in a $16 dish's cost drops a 25% off deal from $7.20 to $6.40 a visit.
  • Change opening hours as well as the deal, so a diner never turns up to a closed door.
  • Pausing takeout is a separate switch from ending a deal.

The short answer

Pause or end a deal when you cannot honour it, when it no longer leaves you enough, or when you will be closed. Do it before the diner arrives, not after, because a diner who has claimed a deal expects it to work for as long as the deal runs.

This post covers the three common reasons, the sum that tells you when a deal has gone thin, and how to handle the people who already claimed.

Three good reasons to stop a deal

  • You cannot serve it. A key ingredient ran out, the kitchen is short a cook, or an event has booked the room. A deal you cannot honour disappoints people, and that is worse than no deal.
  • The numbers changed. An ingredient got dearer, or a deal turned out to be used mostly by people who would have come anyway. Regulars using your deal has that case.
  • You will be closed. A holiday, a staff day or a renovation. A scheduled deal does not know what you intend for the day, so you tell it.

Not every quiet day is a reason to stop. Give a new deal two weeks before judging it, and read the results deal by deal first. Reading your deal results shows the keep, change or stop decision.

When the maths goes thin: a worked example

Take a $16 dish with a food cost of 30% of the menu price, so $4.80 on the plate. These are round example numbers, with no fee to FIG on dine-in.

A 25% off deal when an ingredient gets dearer (example)
BeforeAfter a $0.80 rise
Guest pays (25% off $16)$12.00$12.00
Food cost on the plate$4.80$5.60
You keep toward wages and rent$7.20$6.40

Check: 25% of $16 is $4, so the guest pays $12. $12.00 − $4.80 = $7.20, and $12.00 − $5.60 = $6.40. The deal still leaves money, but $0.80 less on every visit. At 50 visits a week, that is 50 × $0.80 = $40.00 less over the week.

That is a decision, not an emergency. You might move to a smaller dollar amount, swap the dish for one with a lower food cost, or end the deal until prices settle. Pricing a restaurant deal compares the options. Food cost of 28% to 32% of revenue is the range usually quoted, so use your own actual figure.

Pause, change or end

SituationBest moveWhy
One bad night, such as a missing deliveryPause the deal for the night, turn it back on tomorrowYou keep the deal and stop only the promise you cannot keep
A holiday or scheduled closureChange the schedule and your opening hoursNobody turns up to a closed door
The maths has gone thinChange the deal, or end it and post a new oneYou stop losing on every visit
It is simply not workingEnd it after a fair test of two weeksThe slot is open for another idea

If you are not sure which is which, ask what you would be comfortable saying to a diner at the door. If you would be embarrassed to say it, change the deal now.

What happens to diners who already claimed

A claim lasts as long as the deal runs. It ends only when the restaurant ends the deal, or when a flash or dated deal is over. That means that if you end a deal, anyone holding a claim for it can no longer use it, so end a deal deliberately and, where you can, give people a reason to understand.

A few habits that help:

  • Prefer ending at a quiet moment. Not in the middle of a lunch rush when diners are at the door.
  • Put a short note at the door. "This deal has ended. Today's deals are in the FIG app." Staff can say the same.
  • Train the team on one sentence. A diner with an ended claim should hear a kind, clear answer. Train your staff to honour a FIG deal covers how to say it.

Diners see this from their side too. Deal not working at the counter? tells them what to check, so a polite answer from you matches what they have read.

Deals and takeout are separate switches

Ending a dine-in deal does not touch takeout. If you need to slow down kitchen orders, a restaurant can pause takeout and add prep time. Takeout comes with a deal unless the restaurant sets 0 deals per order, in which case takeout is at full price. So if your issue is "the kitchen is slammed", pausing takeout may be the better move, and if it is "the deal is too dear", change the deal. Takeout during a rush has the details.

A short script for staff

When a diner arrives with a claim for a deal that has ended, the person at the door should have one calm sentence ready. Something like: "That deal has ended, I am sorry about that. Here is what is on today." Then point to the live deals or the menu.

Three things make the moment easier. Say sorry once and do not argue about it. Offer what you can do, such as today's live deal. And tell the owner afterwards, so you can see whether the ending was clear enough. If several diners arrive with the same ended deal, the note at the door was not enough, and it is worth putting it up earlier next time.

Start again with a cleaner version

Ending a deal is not a failure. It is a result. Once you have ended one, write down what you learned in one line: the dish, the days, the discount and what went wrong. Then post the next deal with one change. A good second version is often a smaller discount, a shorter window or a different dish.

When you are ready, live now or scheduled helps you choose when the new one should run, and writing a deal diners understand helps with the wording, so the next version is clear from day one.

What it costs

Posting, pausing, changing and ending deals cost a restaurant nothing. FIG earns only on takeout orders paid in the app, keeping 5% of the food after any deal and before tax, with nothing on dine-in.

Questions

How do I know when to end a deal?

End it when you cannot serve it, when it no longer leaves you enough per visit, or when you will be closed. Give a new deal about two weeks and read the results deal by deal before judging it.

What happens to a diner who already claimed a deal I end?

A claim lasts as long as the deal runs, and it ends when the restaurant ends the deal. Tell staff to explain kindly, and consider a note at the door.

Do I need to change my opening hours when I end a deal for a closure?

Yes. Update the opening hours as well as the deal, so a diner does not arrive to a closed door.

Is pausing takeout the same as ending a deal?

No. Takeout can be paused and given more prep time on its own. Takeout comes with a deal unless the restaurant sets 0 deals per order.

Does it cost anything to change or end a deal?

No. Posting, changing and ending deals cost a restaurant nothing.

Can I end a deal and post a new one?

Yes. You can post deals live now or scheduled, with uses per diner and an optional wait between uses.

How do I tell if a deal has gone thin?

Subtract your food cost from what the guest pays. If your food cost rises, as in the $0.80 example, the amount you keep per visit falls by the same amount.
