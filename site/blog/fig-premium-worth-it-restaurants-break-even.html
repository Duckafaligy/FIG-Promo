Is FIG Premium worth $50 a year? A break-even check for restaurants

FIG Premium is $50 CAD a year. A plain way to decide if it is worth it for your restaurant: what it includes, the break-even arithmetic on a plate, and what to measure before you renew.

Key takeaways

  • Posting deals on FIG is free, with no commission on deals. Premium is an extra, at $50 CAD a year.
  • Premium includes unlimited locations, deal announcements every 2 hours, area insights, a menu typed up from a photo and a Premium badge.
  • $50 a year is about $4.17 a month, or about 96 cents a week.
  • In the example below, it takes about 5 extra full-price plates, or 7 plates on a 15%-off deal, to cover a year of Premium.
  • Decide by your own numbers: count what Premium saves in time, add what it brings in diners you can trace, and review it after a quarter.

The short answer

FIG Premium costs $50 CAD a year. It is worth it if it saves you more than $50 of time or brings in enough extra diners to cover that. On a $15 bowl with a 30% food cost, it takes about 5 extra full-price plates in a year to cover $50, or about 7 plates if each one is on a 15%-off deal. Whether Premium delivers that for you is something to measure, not assume. Everything in this post uses round example numbers, so swap in your own.

You do not need Premium to start. Restaurants post deals on FIG free, with no commission on deals. Takeout paid in the app has its own fees, covered below, and they are the same with or without Premium.

Turn $50 into plates

The easiest way to judge any cost is to turn it into the number of plates that pay for it. Contribution is what the diner pays minus the food cost on the plate. It is the money left toward wages, rent and profit.

Break-even on a $15 noodle bowl (example)
How the bowl sellsDiner paysFood cost (30%)ContributionPlates to cover $50
Full price$15.00$4.50$10.505 ($50 ÷ $10.50 = 4.8)
15% off$12.75$4.50$8.257 ($50 ÷ $8.25 = 6.1)

Food cost at 30% is a round example, inside the range often quoted for restaurants. Use your own menu price and food cost. We round up, because you cannot sell part of a plate.

Eight plates in a year is a very small number for a restaurant. The real question is not the arithmetic. It is whether those plates would have been sold anyway. A deal that mostly discounts your regulars is a price cut, not marketing. The slow-hour math covers how to tell the difference.

The same $50, spread out

  • Per month: $50 ÷ 12 = $4.17.
  • Per week: $50 ÷ 52 = about $0.96.
  • Per location, for three locations: $50 ÷ 3 = $16.67 a year each.

The last line is the point of unlimited locations. If you run one place, it does not matter. If you run several, the cost per place falls as you add them, while one $50 covers them all.

The menu-from-a-photo sum

Typing a menu is dull work, and it has a price: your time. If you use the photo feature, FIG types up the menu for you.

Example: time saved on a 40-dish menu
  • Typing a dish, its description and price by hand: about 1.5 minutes each (an example, so time your own).
  • 40 dishes × 1.5 minutes = 60 minutes.
  • If an hour of your time is worth $20 to you, that is $20 of time, a good part of the $50.

You still need to read the result and fix anything wrong before you publish it. That takes a few minutes, and it is much shorter than starting from nothing.

If your menu changes often, or you are adding several locations, the saving repeats. If you have 12 dishes and rarely change them, it is small.

What Premium does not change

  • Fees on takeout. Takeout paid in the app is still 5% of the food before tax, plus Stripe's card fee. On a $20 order after the deal, that is $1.00 to FIG and about $0.96 to Stripe, so you keep about $18.04 of the food. The numbers on four order sizes.
  • The cost of your deals. A 20% deal costs 20% on the plates that use it. Premium does not change that.
  • Your reviews. Reviews come only from diners whose visit was scanned, and you can reply in public.
  • Your diners' privacy. Restaurants never see a diner's phone number.

How to test it without fooling yourself

  1. Write down your baseline. Before you buy Premium, note your weekly scans from your insights and the sales in your quiet hours.
  2. Change one thing at a time. If you add Premium and launch three new deals in the same week, you cannot tell which did what.
  3. Give it a quarter. A week is noise, and seasons matter. Compare like with like.
  4. Count what you can trace. Scans of deals, new diners and diners who came back are in your insights. Extra plates you only hope you got do not count.
  5. Set the rule now. Decide today that you will keep Premium if it clearly covers its $50, and drop it if it does not.

Tax and your books

Think of Premium as a business cost. Whether it is deductible, and how any tax on it is handled, depends on your situation. This is general information, not tax advice, so ask your accountant. If you are not sure whether you need an HST number yet, HST registration for small restaurants explains the basics. Dine-in deals do not need one; takeout paid in the app does.
