Writing a review on FIG: how it works, and what makes one useful

Reviews on FIG come only from scanned visits. What that means, how stars and likes differ, and how to write a few lines that actually help the next person choose.

Key takeaways

  • Reviews come only from scanned visits, so every review on FIG is from someone who was there.
  • Restaurants get stars. Deals get likes. They are two different signals.
  • You need an account to review, and diners must be 14 or older.
  • Early reviews move an average a long way: three reviews of 5, 5 and 1 average 3.7.
  • The useful part is the specifics: what you ordered, when you went, and what you would tell a friend.

The short answer

On FIG you can review a restaurant after your deal has been scanned at that restaurant, which is why every review comes from a real visit. Restaurants get stars and deals get likes, so you can say that the food was good and still say whether the deal itself was worth claiming. A few specific lines about what you ordered beat a long opinion every time.

Who can write a review

Reviews come only from scanned visits. Browsing FIG works as a guest and needs no account, but you need an account to claim, order, save or review. Diners must be 14 or older.

That rule is the whole point. Anyone can type an opinion about a restaurant they have never visited; on FIG the review is attached to a visit where staff scanned your code. If you are reading reviews rather than writing one, how to read a restaurant's reviews on FIG covers what to look for.

Stars, likes, and the difference

What you are ratingHow you say itWhat it tells the next person
The restaurantStarsWhether the place was worth going to
The dealA likeWhether the deal itself was worth claiming

Keeping them separate is useful. A quiet neighbourhood place can have a great kitchen and a thin deal, or a deal worth going out of your way for at a restaurant you thought was fine but not special. If you only had one button, both of those would come out as three stars and nobody would learn anything.

Why your review counts for more than you think

A restaurant that has just gone live on FIG may have very few reviews, so each one moves the average a long way.

How much one review moves an average (example)
  • Three reviews of 5, 5 and 1: 5 + 5 + 1 = 11, and 11 ÷ 3 = 3.67, which shows as 3.7.
  • Add a fourth review of 5: 11 + 5 = 16, and 16 ÷ 4 = 4.0.
  • Now take a restaurant with 12 reviews averaging 4.2, so 12 × 4.2 = 50.4 stars in total.
  • Add one 5: 50.4 + 5 = 55.4, and 55.4 ÷ 13 = 4.26, which shows as 4.3.

These are example numbers. The lesson is the same either way: the first handful of reviews swing an average, and later ones nudge it. If you are the fourth person through the door, your few lines are doing real work.

It cuts both ways, which is a good reason to be fair rather than dramatic when something went wrong once.

What makes a review useful

Four things carry most of the weight: what you ordered, when you went, how long it took, and who it would suit. Everything else is decoration. If you remember the price you paid, say it, because the next reader is usually working out whether the deal was worth the trip.

Writing about the deal itself

Say whether the deal did what you expected. Did it cover the dish you thought it covered? Was it easy at the counter? Did the staff know about it? A line such as "the 2 for 1 covered both mains, no fuss at the till" is genuinely helpful to the next person deciding between two places.

If the deal did not scan, that is worth a sentence too, but check the usual causes first, because most of them have nothing to do with the restaurant. What to check and what to say runs through them.

When the visit went badly

One bad plate is not the same as a bad restaurant, and reviews written in the first ten minutes after a bad meal tend to read that way later. Two habits make a critical review land better:

  • Say what happened, not what you suspect. "The soup was lukewarm and they replaced it" is information. "They clearly do not care" is not.
  • Give the day and time. A slow Saturday night and a slow Tuesday afternoon are very different problems, and an owner can only fix the one they can find.

Restaurants on FIG can reply to reviews, so a fair review often gets an answer, and sometimes a fix. That is the useful end of the process.

What the restaurant sees

Restaurants never see diners' phone numbers. They see the review and can reply to it. Location is optional on FIG and never limits a deal, so you are not sharing where you are to leave a review of where you were.

Your review sits with the restaurant, not with your name on a public feed of everything you have eaten. If you would rather only like a deal and not write anything, that is fine too, and a like is still a signal.

Reviews are how a quiet place gets found

A restaurant that has been in the same plaza for fifteen years may have almost nothing written about it, while a newer place down the road has pages. That gap is not about the food. It is about who happened to write something down.

If you eat somewhere good that nobody seems to know about, a few honest lines are the most useful thing you can do for it, and for the next person looking at the map at six o'clock on a Tuesday wondering where to go. Deals get likes and restaurants get stars, so you can point at both: the place is worth the trip, and the deal is worth claiming.

The same works in reverse. If a deal reads better than it eats, saying so politely saves other people a trip and tells the owner something they can act on.

A short routine

  1. Eat. Let the staff scan your code when you pay.
  2. Before you forget, note the dish and roughly what the bill came to.
  3. Later that evening, open FIG, give the restaurant its stars and write three or four lines.
  4. Like the deal if it was worth claiming.
  5. If you would go back, say so in a sentence. That is the line most readers look for.

Questions

Who can leave a review on FIG?

Only diners whose visit was scanned at the restaurant. You need an account to review, and diners must be 14 or older.

What is the difference between stars and likes?

Restaurants get stars. Deals get likes. One says whether the place was worth going to, the other whether the deal was worth claiming.

Can a restaurant reply to my review?

Yes. Restaurants can read and reply to reviews.

Does the restaurant see my phone number?

No. Restaurants never see diners' phone numbers.

Can I review a restaurant I did not visit?

No. Reviews come only from scanned visits.

Do I have to share my location to review?

No. Location is optional on FIG and never limits a deal.

How long should a review be?

Three or four lines is plenty: what you ordered, when you went, how long it took, and whether you would go back.
