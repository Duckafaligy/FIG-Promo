A 'free with a dish' deal: what it costs you, with the math

Give a drink, a side or a dessert with a main, and the real cost is your food cost, not your menu price. A worked example, how to choose the item, and how it compares with a dollar off.

Key takeaways

  • On FIG a deal can be 2 for 1, percent off, dollar off, or free with a dish.
  • With a free item, the cost to you is what the item costs to make, not what it sells for.
  • Choose an item with a high menu price and a low food cost, such as a drink or a side, and one that fits the main.
  • Compare it with a dollar-off deal using the same arithmetic: what does one use leave you?
  • Start with a quiet window, a limit on uses per diner and two weeks of results on your dashboard.

The short answer

A free-with-a-dish deal gives the diner an extra item when they order a dish. It costs you what that item costs to make, not the price on your menu, so a drink or a side can be a cheap way to bring people in. Work out the food cost of the item, add it to the cost of the main, and check what each use leaves you.

What the deal is

On FIG, a restaurant can post a deal as 2 for 1, percent off, dollar off, or free with a dish. The last one is a pairing: the diner orders a dish and gets another item with it. You choose the dish, the extra item, the hours, how many times one diner can use it and how long they wait between uses. You can post it live now or scheduled. Which type suits which restaurant compares all four.

Why the cost is not the menu price

If a drink sells for $3.50, giving it away does not cost you $3.50. It costs the ingredients, the cup and the few seconds of staff time. A dollar-off deal is different: $3.00 off is $3.00 gone from the till, every time. That is why a free item can look generous to the diner while costing you much less than the same amount taken off the bill.

The catch is that this only works for items with a low food cost. A steak and a soda are very different things to give away.

A worked example

These are round example numbers, not a real restaurant. A main sells for $16.00 and costs $4.80 to make. A drink sells for $3.50 and costs $0.70 to make. The deal is dine-in, so FIG takes nothing from the bill.

One use of a free drink with a $16 main (example)
LineWorkingAmount
Main at full price$16.00 − $4.80$11.20 left
Main with a free drink$16.00 − $4.80 − $0.70$10.50 left
Main with $3.50 off instead$16.00 − $3.50 − $4.80$7.70 left
Difference, free drink vs $3.50 off$10.50 − $7.70$2.80 more for you

The diner sees a $3.50 drink for nothing, and you give up $0.70. The $3.50 off deal costs you the full $3.50. If the diner would have bought the drink anyway, the picture changes: the drink was a sale at $3.50 with $2.80 left after its cost, and now you earn nothing from it. That is the same question as in reading your deal results: who would have come, and what would they have bought, anyway?

Choosing the item

Good choiceWhy
A fountain drink, tea or coffeeLow food cost, quick to serve
A small side or a soupEasy to prepare in batches
A small dessertGives the diner a reason to stay and tells them what else you make
A topping or an upgradeLow cost, and diners remember it

Avoid items that are expensive to make, slow to prepare or that you often run short of. If the free item is out, the deal is out, and a diner who was promised a dessert and told it is gone is not a happy diner.

Choosing the main

Pair the free item with a main that you want to sell more of, one with a good margin that is easy for the kitchen. A deal on your slowest, least profitable dish is hard to justify. A deal on a popular dish that is already in the kitchen's rhythm is easy to serve and easy to repeat.

Name the pairing clearly, so the diner and the staff both know what it is. A short description is enough.

When it beats the other types

  • Against dollar off: better when the extra item has a low food cost, as in the example above.
  • Against percent off: better when you want to protect the price of the main. The menu price stays the same.
  • Against 2 for 1: better when you want to bring in one diner rather than two.

It is worse when the item costs a lot to make, or when most diners would have ordered it anyway.

Watch for the common mistakes

  • Choosing an item the kitchen cannot spare. If the free item is the one that is always running out, pick another.
  • Forgetting the main's cost. The deal is only worth running if the main still leaves you something. In the example, $10.50 is left after the main and the free drink. If a main only leaves $3.00 after its cost, a free item of any size can wipe it out.
  • Giving the same item to everyone all day. A deal for a quiet window brings people in when you need them. The same deal at the lunch rush only gives away items to people who were already coming.
  • Not telling the team. A free item that the server did not know about is a free item argued about at the counter.

A second example: a free dessert

Here is another round-number example, to show that the same steps work for a different item. A main sells for $18.00 and costs $5.40 to make. A dessert sells for $7.00 and costs $1.40 to make. With the dessert free, what is left from the main is $18.00 − $5.40 − $1.40 = $11.20. Without the deal and without the dessert it is $12.60. So a diner who would have ordered only the main costs you $1.40 for that use. A diner who was never going to come at all brings you $11.20. If the deal brings in even a few diners like that on a quiet afternoon, it can pay for the cost of the free desserts given to others.

Setting it up

  1. Choose a quiet window, using the slow-hour math.
  2. Do the arithmetic above with your own food costs.
  3. Set the uses per diner. One is a good start. If you want regulars to come back, uses per diner and the wait between uses shows how.
  4. Brief your team, so everyone knows what the free item is and how to scan the code. Train your staff in 10 minutes.
  5. Check the results after two weeks.

If the deal comes with takeout, remember the takeout fees: FIG keeps 5% of the food after any deal and before tax, on takeout orders only, and Stripe's card fee comes off the payment. Takeout order math has the arithmetic.

Questions

What does 'free with a dish' mean on FIG?

It is one of four deal types, alongside 2 for 1, percent off and dollar off. The diner orders a dish and gets another item with it.

What does a free item cost me?

What it costs to make, not its menu price. In the example, a $3.50 drink that costs $0.70 to make costs you $0.70.

Which items work best as the free item?

Items with a low food cost that are quick to serve, such as drinks, small sides, soup or a small dessert.

Can I limit how often a diner uses it?

Yes. You set uses per diner, and an optional wait between uses. A diner can claim a deal once a day.

Does FIG take a cut of a dine-in deal?

No. FIG takes nothing on dine-in. It earns only on takeout orders paid in the app.

How do I know whether the deal is paying off?

Read the results for that deal on your dashboard and compare them with your own till and your food costs, after at least two weeks.

Does it cost anything to post a deal?

No. Posting deals costs restaurants nothing.
