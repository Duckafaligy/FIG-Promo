Writing a deal diners understand: wording, limits and the small print

Most arguments at the till come from a missing word, not from a diner trying it on. What to write in a deal so diners, and your own team, read it the same way.

Key takeaways

  • Say four things: what the diner gets, which dishes it covers, when it runs, and what it leaves out.
  • A diner decides before the scan, from the words you wrote, so write for someone who has never been in.
  • On a 2 for 1, say whether the cheaper dish is the one that comes off. The example below shows an $8 difference.
  • Match the words to the settings: live now or scheduled, uses per diner and the wait between uses.
  • If a diner reads it another way, serve it that day and fix the wording the same evening.

The short answer

Write the deal the way you would explain it to someone standing at your till: what they get, on which dishes, when it runs, and anything it does not cover. A deal that reads clearly gets used the way you meant it, and your team never has to guess. Most arguments at the counter come from a missing word rather than from a diner trying it on.

What a diner sees before they claim

A diner finds your deal on the map or on your page, reads it, and claims it in one tap. Later they show a QR code and your staff scan it at the restaurant. Everything the diner decides happens before that scan, from the few lines you wrote.

Those lines do three jobs at once. They sell the deal, they set what the diner expects to be served, and they tell your own team what to put on the plate. If one of the three is missing, you find out in the middle of a shift. How a deal works from claim to counter walks through the diner's side of it.

The four things every deal should say

The question a diner hasWhat to writeExample wording
What do I get?The deal in plain words, with the amount$4 off your bill
On what?The dishes or the menu section it coversOn any main from the noodle section
When?The days and hours, if it is not obviousTuesday to Thursday, 2 pm to 5 pm
What is not in it?The things people will ask aboutDrinks and desserts are not included

FIG already shows the deal type you chose, whether it is a 2 for 1, a percent off, a dollar off or a free item with a dish, along with the days and hours and how many times a diner can use it. Your words fill the gaps that only you know: which dishes, which sizes, and what a busy server should do when someone asks.

Words that start arguments

  • "Main" without a list. Your menu may have twelve mains at nine prices. Name the section, or name the dishes.
  • "Free drink" on its own. Which drink, and what size? Write "a 12 oz house drink" or whatever yours is.
  • Sizes left out. If the deal is on the small bowl, say small bowl.
  • Two deals at once. If two of your deals could land on the same visit, write which one applies so the person at the till is not deciding alone.
  • Numbers that do not match the till. If the menu price on your page is old, the deal reads as a bigger saving than it is. Keeping your page current covers that.

Each diner claims a deal on their own phone and shows their own code, so write the deal as it applies to one person's order. If you mean a table of four to get one deal between them, that is different from four people each claiming, and the words have to say so.

A missing word, in numbers

A 2 for 1 with and without the rule written down (example)
  • Two diners order a $22 main and a $14 main. The bill at menu prices is $36.
  • If the cheaper dish is the one that comes off, they pay $22 and you give away $14.
  • If the dearer dish comes off, they pay $14 and you give away $22.
  • The difference between those two readings is $22 − $14 = $8 on one table.

These are example prices. Eight dollars is not the end of the world once, but twenty of those in a month is $160, and it is the sort of thing that ends up in a review. One line in the deal, such as "the lower priced main is the one that comes off", settles it before anyone orders.

The same goes for a percent-off deal on a large order. If you do not want 20% coming off a $200 group bill, say which dishes or which part of the bill it covers.

Match the words to the settings

Your wording should agree with what you set in the app. A deal can be live now or scheduled, and you choose how many times one diner can use it, up to 20, with an optional wait between uses.

  • If you set a wait between uses, the words can say "once a week" or whatever the wait is, so a regular is not surprised.
  • If you scheduled the deal for an afternoon, you do not need to repeat the hours in the text, but it helps a diner planning ahead.
  • If the deal is for one use only, you can leave that alone. A deal code works once.

Live now or scheduled covers the timing, and uses per diner and the wait between uses covers repeat visits.

Read it back as three different people

Before you post, read the deal three times, as three people:

  1. A diner who has never been in. Do they know what they would be ordering and roughly what they would pay?
  2. A new server on a Saturday. Could they serve it correctly without finding a manager?
  3. You, at the end of a long shift. Is there any reading of it that costs you more than you meant?

If any of the three stumbles, the fix is usually one extra sentence, not a different deal.

A checklist before you post

  1. The deal amount is in the text, in dollars or per cent.
  2. The dishes or the menu section are named.
  3. The days and hours read the same as the schedule you set.
  4. Anything excluded is written down: drinks, desserts, sides, sizes.
  5. Your team has heard the deal in the same words. Train your staff in 10 minutes has a short script.
  6. The menu prices on your page match the till.

It is a five-minute job, and it costs nothing to post a deal: signing up, setting up the page, going live, posting deals, scanning and the dashboard cost nothing for restaurants.

If a diner reads it differently

It happens. Someone claims the deal, reads it one way, and your team reads it another. The cheapest answer is almost always to serve it the diner's way that day, then fix the wording that evening. The cost of one plate is lower than the cost of an argument in the dining room, and reviews on FIG come only from scanned visits, so the people writing them are the ones who were there.

Then change the words, not the whole deal. Change one thing at a time, so you can tell what made a difference. Replying to reviews has a short guide for the ones that mention a deal.

If the deal is for takeout as well

Takeout orders come with a deal unless you set 0 deals per order, in which case takeout is at full price. The wording matters more here, because nobody is standing in front of your team to ask. Spell out the dishes, and keep the takeout wording identical to the dine-in wording if the deal is the same. Takeout during a rush covers deals per order, pausing and prep time.

Questions

What should a deal description include?

What the diner gets, which dishes it covers, when it runs, and anything it does not cover, such as drinks or desserts.

Do I have to write the days and hours in the text?

No. You set the days and hours when you schedule the deal, and FIG shows them. Repeating them in the text helps a diner planning ahead.

How do I word a 2 for 1 fairly?

Say which of the two dishes comes off. Writing that the lower priced dish is the one that comes off settles it before anyone orders.

Can one diner use my deal more than once?

Yes, if you allow it. You set uses per diner, up to 20, with an optional wait between uses. A deal code works once, and the diner's screen shows a new code after each scan.

What if my team serves the deal wrongly?

Serve the diner's reading that day, then fix the wording the same evening and tell the team in the same words.

Does it cost anything to post a deal?

No. Signing up, setting up the page, going live, posting deals, scanning and the dashboard cost nothing for restaurants.

Does the same wording work for takeout?

Keep it identical if the deal is the same. Takeout comes with a deal unless you set 0 deals per order, and nobody is there to ask you a question.
